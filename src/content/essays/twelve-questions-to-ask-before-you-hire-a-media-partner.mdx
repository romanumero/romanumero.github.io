Every media partner I compete with pitches its technology now: automation, proprietary platforms, and lately AI. So does KORTX.

That's the problem with pitches. They all say the same things, and none of it is hard to put on a slide. <mark>What's hard to fake is what happens on an ordinary Tuesday afternoon when a campaign breaks, the person who set it up is out, and the client is asking why spend stopped.</mark>

So if you're choosing a media partner, or reviewing the one you have, skip the capabilities slide. Ask questions whose answers can't be written in advance.

Here are the twelve I'd ask. Ask them of us too. For a few of them, I've included our own answer.

## How to use this list

Ask for examples, not capabilities. "We can do that" isn't an answer. "Last month, this happened, and here's what we did" is.

Listen for specifics: a name, a number, a date, a thing that went wrong. Vague answers aren't lies, but they usually mean nobody on the other side of the table has looked closely.

And notice the pauses. A partner that has to think before answering "who in this room will actually run my account?" has just answered it.

## Is the technology doing the work, or selling it?

### 1. What did your technology take off your team's plate last quarter?

Technology that only shows up during the pitch doesn't change your campaigns. The test is whether it changed how the work gets done after the contract is signed. Whether that's a simple rule, a script, an integration or an AI model matters much less than whether it runs every week.

**A good answer** names a specific task, what now handles it, roughly how many hours it saved, and what the team does with that time now.

**A red flag** is an answer about how fast they can build a proposal, not how they run a live account.

### 2. Show me one step that runs without a person. What catches it when it's wrong?

Automation without a check just makes mistakes faster. You want both halves: the step that runs by itself, and the thing that notices when it goes wrong.

**A good answer** is concrete. For example: campaign settings are built in the buying platform automatically, then checked line by line against the approved plan before launch.

**A red flag** is "our platform handles optimization," or "our AI does," with no named check behind it.

### 3. What's still done by hand, and why?

Any partner that's genuinely building this stuff knows exactly where the gaps are, because it runs into them every day.

**A good answer** is a specific list with reasons: this step still needs judgment, that one isn't reliable enough yet, this one we're testing by hand before we automate it.

**A red flag** is "nothing, it's all automated."

Our own answer: we decide stage by stage. A person runs our plan-scoring tool and reviews the result. Ad group builds are automated, geography settings have to pass a check before a campaign can move forward, and our [setup audits are moving from AI skills into software](/essays/easy-to-build-hard-to-run/). Every step climbs the same ladder: define it, run it by hand, then automate it.

### 4. When your automation breaks, how do you find out?

It will break. Platforms change their settings, data feeds go stale, a rule meets a case nobody planned for. What matters is who notices first.

**A good answer** describes [monitoring that raises an alert to a named owner](/essays/which-agency-workflow-should-you-automate-first/), and includes a recent example of it working.

**A red flag** is "it doesn't break," or "you'd let us know."

## Who is actually doing the work?

### 5. Which of the people in this room will actually run my account after we sign?

This is the question I'd ask first. In a lot of pitches, the people who win the business disappear the week after the contract is signed, and your account is handed to a team you've never met.

**A good answer** points to the people already at the table, names their roles on your account, and tells you how many other accounts they carry.

**A red flag** is a room full of salespeople and executives, with the actual account team "to be assigned after kickoff."

### 6. How many handoffs sit between me and the person who changes the campaign settings?

Every handoff adds delay, and every handoff is a place where a detail gets lost. In my experience, most campaign errors start one or two handoffs before anyone notices them.

**A good answer** is one or two handoffs, and the partner can sketch them for you.

**A red flag** is that they can't tell you, or the answer depends on the time of day.

### 7. How long has the team on my account done this work, and how long have they been with you?

Your account's history lives in people's heads: why the geo targeting is set up that way, which approvals the client's legal team insists on, what went wrong last spring. Turnover erases it.

**A good answer** gives specific tenure, names the people, and explains how coverage works when someone is out.

**A red flag** is "our team" with no names attached.

Our own answer: four in ten people at KORTX have been here seven years or more, and [having the same people show up month after month](/essays/nobody-has-ever-thanked-us-for-a-dashboard/) is a big reason clients stay. Instead of leaving that experience in a static doc, we codify our standard operating procedures directly into the workflows of our operating system, so the steps, checks and account context live right where the work happens. Ask a partner with a long-tenured team how their processes are built into the way they work, not just who holds them in their head.

### 8. What's your median response time, and how do you know?

Few partners measure this. Ask anyway. How they react tells you whether anyone is watching.

**A good answer** is a number the partner actually tracks.

**A red flag** is "we're very responsive."

## Can you prove it?

### 9. Who checks your numbers besides you?

A score a partner calculates, names and grades itself on tells you how the partner sees itself. It doesn't tell you what your media did.

**A good answer** points to sources you can check yourself: your own analytics, third-party verification, a test design you could repeat without them.

**A red flag** is a proprietary score you can't reproduce, reported only by the partner that benefits from it.

### 10. When platform numbers aren't enough, how do you measure what the media actually drove?

Sometimes standard platform reporting is all a campaign needs, and there's nothing wrong with that when it fits the goal. The problem is when a partner treats platform-reported conversions as the only way to measure performance, even when a client is asking bigger questions about incremental lift or where the next dollar should go.

**A good answer** meets you where you are: clear platform reporting when that's what you're looking for, and deeper analysis—such as holdout groups, lift tests or marketing mix modeling—when you need to know what would have happened without the media. I laid out how we step through those levels in the [MAGIC framework](/essays/magic-framework/).

**A red flag** is a partner that only has one gear, and presents platform-reported numbers as the final word for every client.

### 11. Tell me about a time a test told you something you didn't want to hear.

This is the honesty test. Anyone running real experiments has results that embarrassed a plan, a channel, or their own recommendation.

**A good answer** is a specific story that ends in a changed decision.

**A red flag** is no story at all.

## What happens when it goes wrong?

### 12. Tell me about your last make-good. What changed afterward?

Every partner makes mistakes. I've never met one that didn't. What matters is whether the same mistake happens twice.

**A good answer** names the root cause, where in their process it actually started, the fix they made at that point, and how they checked the fix held.

**A red flag** is blaming the platform or the client, or claiming make-goods basically don't happen.

Our own answer: every issue we review gets two tags, where it was caught and where it started, and the fix goes where it started.

## Turn the list around

If you run an agency, your clients are starting to ask you versions of these same questions, especially the ones about technology and automation.

So try it. Could your team answer all twelve today, with a name, a number or a recent example? Which ones would you have to bluff?

<mark>Those are the places to start. Not because a competitor will expose them, but because they're the same places your campaigns quietly lose money.</mark>
